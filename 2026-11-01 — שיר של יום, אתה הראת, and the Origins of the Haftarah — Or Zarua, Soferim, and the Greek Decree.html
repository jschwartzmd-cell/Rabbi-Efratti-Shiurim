Rabbi Ephrati · Sunday Morning שיעור on תפילה

שיר של יום, אתה הראת, and the Origins of the Haftarah — Or Zarua, Soferim, and the Greek Decree

From the Levites' song of the day to הוצאת ספר תורה, the מי שברך after each עלייה, and a clash between Rashi's daily reading of the Prophets and the Avudraham's Greek decree
Recorded 1 November 2026 · 21 Cheshvan 5787 · Reworked from the original שיעור

The shiur finishes Shacharis of Shabbos with the שיר של יום and asks why Ashkenazim say it after Musaf when the Levites sang it with the morning offering. It then moves to taking out the Torah: the honor of opening the ark, the verses of מסכת סופרים, the אור זרוע's dream about the spelling of עקיבא, why we say אתה הראת on Shabbos, the מי שברך after each עלייה, and the two ancient explanations of the haftarah: Rashi's daily reading of a chapter of Prophets, and the Avudraham's response to a Greek decree.

I. Dedication, and the Placement of Shir Shel Yom

The Rav opens by noting that today's breakfast and shiur are sponsored by a dear friend of the shul in honor of his mother's יארצייט, מרים בת חיים יצחק, and asks that her נשמה have an עלייה, and that it bring a ישועה for all Jews. He reminds the room of the Gemara's rule תרי קלי לא משתמעי, two voices cannot be heard at once, and the shiur begins.

The series is now learning the תפילות של שבת קודש. Shacharis of Shabbos is finished, and there is one more thing to complete before moving on to ליינען: the שיר של יום. Every morning we say "היום יום ראשון בשבת" and so on; on Shabbos we say "היום יום שבת קודש, שבו היו הלוים אומרים בבית המקדש" and then מזמור שיר ליום השבת.[1] The Rav asks when we say it. Nusach Sefard says it after Shacharis, and the Sefardim have already said מזמור שיר in פסוקי דזמרה; Ashkenazim also say it there, yet נוסח ספרד goes out of its way to say the song of the day again after Shacharis. Traditional נוסח אשכנז, by contrast, says it after Musaf.

"It's a peleh": why after Musaf?

The Rav calls this practice a פלא. Why do we say a שיר של יום at all? We learned this when we learned the weekday Tefillah: when the Beis HaMikdash stood, Shacharis corresponds to the תמיד של שחר. After the תמיד של שחר the Kohanim brought the נסכים that accompany every קרבן תמיד, and while they did, the לויים stood על הדוכן and sang the song of the day. So the שיר של יום is tied to the morning offering, hence to Shacharis, and there is no reason to wait for Musaf. On the contrary: whenever Musaf offerings were brought (Rosh Chodesh, Shabbos, Yom Tov), the Levites sang a special song for the קרבן מוסף. So the מגן אברהם says the Ashkenazic custom of saying the daily song after Musaf makes no sense; after Musaf there is a different song.[2] And, the Rav adds, we do not say that Musaf song today, and the ערוך השלחן wonders why.

He puts the question to the room: if every תמיד של שחר comes with its song, why, when we daven Musaf, which corresponds to the קרבן מוסף, do we not say the song the Levites sang for it? "The ערוך השלחן has no answer." Someone offers an answer, and the Rav points out that מסכת סופרים, at the back of מסכת עבודה זרה, spells out the songs for every Musaf: Rosh Chodesh, Shabbos, Yom Tov, Shavuos, Sukkos, Pesach.[3] "Every time there's a Musaf, they say, now we can learn Torah" is how the Rav (somewhat tongue in cheek) summarizes the point before taking his customary sixty-second pause, "because I need a coffee."

Nitra, Young Israel of Woodmere, and the decorum committee

The Rav returns: the מגן אברהם holds that the Sefardic way, שיר של יום after Shacharis, is correct. A congregant mentioned that Nitra, a shul in Borough Park, has changed to saying it after Shacharis, and the Rav marvels at this: Nitra's rabbi wears a שטריימל, the shul is very Ashkenazic, אויבערלאנד אשכנזיש, and yet they changed. (He notes that in Vienna, he does not think they changed anything; "they don't change anything.")[4]

Then he shares something he "just learned this morning." Thirty years ago, at the Young Israel of Woodmere, the rabbi at the time, Rabbi Billet, moved both the שיר של יום and אנעים זמירות from after Musaf to after Shacharis. It was not for the reason the Magen Avraham gives. It was because the decorum committee decided that after Musaf there was too much בלאגן: kidding around, שמועס, and everybody folding up their טליתים before davening was over. "It's not after דאווענען yet! You didn't finish davening. What are you doing? Talk to the אייבערשטער." Moving the songs earlier lets them be said with the proper respect and dignity. The Rav jokes that the shul, Beis Yeshayah, has no such committee but might form one, and asks for volunteers.

Why not after Mincha?

One more question: if in the Beis HaMikdash the תמיד של בין הערבים also had נסכים with ניסוך היין, and the Levites sang during the wine libation, why do we not say a שיר של יום every day after Mincha? "A מוראדיקע קשיא." The מגן אברהם answers, and the Rav calls it "כדאי to know": at the תמיד של בין הערבים the Temple was so hectic, with so many offerings, that the wine libation sometimes took place after שקיעה. The halachah is that when the libation falls after sunset, the Levites no longer sing at night. Since the song might therefore not have been said, we do not say it at Mincha. A congregant suggests that the songs are simply "designated for the day," so the Musaf song would not be a שיר של יום; the Rav answers that we could still add the Musaf song, but he hears the point and moves on.

II. Taking Out the Sefer Torah — Who Opens the Ark?

The Rav turns to taking out the ספר תורה. Much of this was discussed when the series covered Monday and Thursday, but Shabbos differs from the weekday. First, a custom of this shul that "not every shul has": on שמחת תורה, when the כיבודים are sold, someone buys the right to take out the Torah for the whole year. (The purchaser is named on the recording; he sometimes gives it up for a חתן, or for a man whose wife is expecting.)[5]

This custom has a source. The אור זרוע, one of the great ראשונים, was the Rav of Vienna, רבינו יצחק בר משה, who wrote a sefer of that name in the 1200s, a very important work recording a great many halachos. He writes that it is a big זכות to purchase the הוצאת ספר תורה, and כבוד התורה.

The expectant father in the ninth month

What is the source for giving the honor to a man whose wife is in her ninth month? The חיד"א brings this as a מנהג ותיקין, an ancient custom. The idea is symbolic: she needs פתיחת הרחם at the birth, so he opens the ארון, and when you open the ark it is a זמן מסוגל, an עת רצון to daven.[6] The Rav's practice: when he sees a young man going up for פתיחה (and the community generally knows whose wife is expecting) he stands right by the ארון קודש and tells him: "Don't just open it and take out the Torah. Daven! Daven that you should have children, that the child should be a בן תורה and learn Torah; and if it's a girl, that she should marry a תלמיד חכם."

Why does a חתן get פתיחה? The Rav asks the room. Someone suggests it is the same idea, an auspicious moment; another suggests הכשר מצוה. "קען זיין," the Rav says, and moves on.

III. Which Verses? Masechet Soferim and the Customs of Eretz Yisrael

"The עולם knows that I like researching what the historical background is behind everything in the סידור." During the week, when the Torah is taken out, we say קל ארך אפים, or a couple of verses such as אין כמוך, and then ויהי בנסוע הארן. On Shabbos we say אתה הראת לדעת כי ה' הוא האלקים אין עוד מלבדו and other verses that differ from the weekday ones. Where does this come from?

Masechet Soferim — scribes, minhagim, and an unsolved puzzle

There is, in the back of עבודה זרה, among the מסכתות קטנות, the tractate מסכת סופרים, "the tractate of the scribes." It contains many חידושים that do not appear in the Bavli or Yerushalmi, and "all the great academics till today are still struggling to figure out when it was written, by whom it was authored, when it was first published and disseminated, and where the מנהגים come from." The Rav is in the middle of reading a book by a professor in Eretz Yisrael on the subject, and from what he has read over the past several years he thinks that מסכת סופרים is primarily the ancient מנהגים של ארץ ישראל, which stood in a big מחלוקת with the מנהגים של בבל.[7]

In Eretz Yisrael, it seems, they said many different verses when taking the Torah out of the ark. "If we would do what מסכת סופרים says whenever you take out a Torah on Shabbos and Yom Tov, you would be in shul an extra hour." Not only that: they had many more verses than our סידור, each one said by the חזן and then the קהל, some verses said by the חזן and repeated twice by the congregation. It is "a lengthy chapter" that would literally take a half hour to an hour. בריך שמיה does not appear there at all; it comes from the זוהר הקדוש, as discussed when the series learned בריך שמיה. "ברוך השם, we don't have it today." We have a much abbreviated version. Some of our verses are in מסכת סופרים; some were added. ויהי בנסוע הארן, which we say every time we take out a Torah, is not there: "it's a new thing."

The Or Zarua's dream — how a gittin question named a sefer

Where does our practice come from? From the אור זרוע, whom the Rav describes as one of the בעלי התוספות and the chief rabbi of Vienna. Why is the sefer called אור זרוע? The Rav asks a member of the room how he spells his name, עקיבא: with an אלף at the end. Now, נישט מער געדאכט, when you write a גט and the man's name is עקיבא, how do you write it, with an אלף or a ה'?

The אור זרוע was writing a גט for a man named עקיבא and wanted to know. Throughout the תלמוד ירושלמי and the תוספתא, which originate in Eretz Yisrael, the name is written עקיבה with a ה'. Throughout the Bavli and the משניות, "רבי עקיבא אומר" is with an אלף. A גט with one letter off is פסול, and "we're talking about an אשת איש, it's not פשוט."[8]

So, he writes in the introduction to his book, he had a dream. מן השמים they showed him a verse: אור זרוע לצדיק ולישרי לב שמחה. Take the סופי תיבות, the last letters of the words, and you get רבי עקיבא, but since שמחה ends with a ה', the last letter of עקיבא as represented by this verse is a ה'. "So he said, thank you, מן השמים, this is how I'm going to write the גט." And he loved the revelation so much that he named the sefer אור זרוע to remind himself. To this a congregant answers with תורה לא בשמים היא, and the Rav says "very good."

Halachah lema'aseh today? The Rav notes there is a global expert in writing גיטין, Rabbi Moshe Langer, the son of a dear friend of the shul, and he has never had the chance to ask him what the practice is. He thinks some פוסקים are מחמיר and write two גיטין, one with an אלף and one with a ה'. "It's not נוגע for us, ברוך השם. You should be זוכה to שלום בית and לאנגע געזונטע יארן." A similar question arises with פנחס, with or without a יוד ("I have a son פנחס, so it's נוגע"), and with the names of men and women in גיטין generally; in Europe, a participant notes, they added יודים, אלפים, עינים and ואוים. That is beyond the scope of the shiur, but "it's not so פשוט; he wrote a whole sefer about this. It's a huge מחלוקת."[9]

The Rhine, the Shibbolei HaLeket, and the four exiled families

Why mention all this? Because "what we do today every time we take out a Torah comes from the אור זרוע." He writes that he traveled a great deal, to אשכנז and to Poland, in the 1200s. In the region he calls מדינת ריינוס (the Rhine River), they said nothing when taking out the Torah: after Shacharis they walked up to the ark, took out the scroll, brought it to the בימה, and began to read. The אור זרוע writes that our מנהג should follow the ancient מנהג ארץ ישראל of מסכת סופרים, and he abbreviates it, giving only several verses; that is what we say.[10]

The Rav adds a note on another ראשון: the שבלי הלקט, whom the בית יוסף quotes very often, especially in the laws of prayer. Its author, as the Rav recalls it ("רב אברהם בן צדקי, I think צדקיהו בן אברהם הרופא"), was the son of a doctor, רב אברהם הרופא, a major פוסק, רב and ראש ישיבה in Rome. The family belonged to the משפחת הענוים, and this is "Jewish history" worth knowing.[11]

The four families exiled to Rome There is a legend, recorded by the ראשונים, that at the חורבן בית שני Titus exiled many Jews, and among them four very prominent families from the nobility of Jerusalem. (The Rav begins "to Bavel," then corrects himself: "I'm sorry, he exiled them to Rome.") The four were משפחת האדומים, משפחת התפוחים, משפחת הענוים, and a fourth ("four special families"; the fourth name is not given on the recording). Many of our ראשונים and פוסקים came from those families, and many הלכות we follow rest on sefarim written by their members. The פוסקים take them so seriously because they have a direct tradition from the ancient customs of Eretz Yisrael: "They came from the חורבן בית שני, and they went and they never changed. They lived in Rome for hundreds of years."

Rav Avraham the doctor, living in Rome, saw that in Italy, as in the rest of Ashkenaz, nothing was said when the Torah was taken out, and he said: let us bring back the ancient custom of מסכת סופרים. "Hence, from that point on in history, כלל ישראל all over" says these verses. (The Rav is not sure what the Sefardim do exactly; "I think they say more verses.") As for who composed the verses, they are all verses of the תנ"ך, from דוד המלך in תהלים and elsewhere.

IV. Atah Hareisa Lada'as and Vayehi Binsoa HaAron

On Shabbos we begin with אתה הראת לדעת. Why on Shabbos and not during the week, when we say קל ארך אפים? קל ארך אפים is about מחילה and סליחה, "a וואכעדיגע זאך," a weekday matter. אתה הראת לדעת is said of the Torah, at מתן תורה, in פרשת ואתחנן. Since the Torah was given on Shabbos, before taking out the scroll on Shabbos we say אתה הראת to commemorate that fact.[12] The Rav points to the טור in סימן רפ"א.

אַתָּה הָרְאֵתָ לָדַעַת כִּי יְהוָה הוּא הָאֱלֹהִים אֵין עוֹד מִלְּבַדּוֹ

The Rav then adds a wrinkle in נוסח אשכנז: "They don't say אתה הראת at all." The Tur lived in Spain for the second half of his life, and writes that the Spanish custom is to say it. We adopted that custom in נוסח ספרד; נוסח אשכנז does not (the Rav concedes he has not davened נוסח אשכנז for a long time and confirms with the room). All the other verses are left out on Shabbos.

After that comes ויהי בנסע הארן. As noted, it is not in מסכת סופרים, but it is very fitting: we are taking the Torah out of the ark, בנסע הארן, and the ark is moving.[13] Then בריך שמיה, which was discussed in the past.

V. The Mi Sheberach After Every Aliyah

A question from "a couple of weeks ago." On Shabbos, after every עלייה, we say a מי שברך. On Monday and Thursday there is none (aside from a מי שברך for a יולדת or someone sick). Where does it come from, that we stop in the middle of the reading? The Rav answers: "For money."

If you look in the Midrash, in ויקרא רבה and קהלת רבה, there was an ancient custom that, in the merit of the Torah open on the בימה, one gave the man who received the עלייה a blessing: מי שברך אבותינו אברהם יצחק ויעקב. The point of the מי שברך, he says, is indeed the מתנה, the pledge.[14]

A sober warning about pledges The Midrash says that one who pledges charity publicly (פוסק צדקה ברבים) and, חס ושלום, does not keep his word and does not pay the shul the money he promised at the מי שברך: "I don't want to say what the Midrash says because it's very scary." The Gemara says בעוון נדרים אשתו ובניו מתים, השם ירחם; if someone makes a נדר, especially a public one, and does not fulfil it, he violates מוצא שפתיך תשמור and לא יחל דברו ככל היוצא מפיו יעשה. (The Rav ascribes this to the Gemara in Nedarim; see the note.) So the גבאי should always add בלי נדר.

The Rav adds a point of practice. When the גבאי gives him an עלייה and recites בעבור שצוה לברכם, he tells him: No. The text should be בעבור שיתן מתנה to the בית הכנסת, "otherwise there's no point to the מי שברך"; בעבור שצוה לברכם ("because he commanded") is how rabbis think they can "get away" with it. "That was the original מנהג, and I try to fix him all the time. And that's why my bill in the shul is..." The Rav jokes about his own outstanding balance.

The Mi Sheberach for the sick on Shabbos

A question was raised: we make a מי שברך לחולים, and the Rav gave it special attention in the shul. A woman from the עזרת נשים once phoned him during the week to say that she comes to shul on Shabbos morning looking forward to the מי שברך לחולים. She has a whole list of sick people she davens for; she comes specially for that moment, "not just for the davening and the reading, and definitely not for the rabbi's speech." And she asked why everyone is talking at that time. He took it seriously and told the גבאי, who now bangs and announces גיב אכט before the מי שברך לחולים: people wait a whole week to say a מי שברך when the Torah is open, so that the זכות התורה should bring the רפואה.

But how can we ask for healing on Shabbos? There is no רפאנו in the Shabbos Amidah, as discussed last week: one is not to make individual requests on Shabbos, because it is "as if כל מלאכתך עשויה," the mode of a Jew on Shabbos is יום שכולו שבת, יום שכולו מנוחה לחיי העולמים. The בית יוסף (the author of the שולחן ערוך) was asked exactly this and answered in a responsum in אבקת רוכל: a מי שברך is not a תפילה for the sick; it is a ברכה. We are simply letting the family know that we are thinking of the patient and that the merit of the Torah should bring healing; it is "a simple blessing," not a request. And, the Rav concludes, הנח להם לישראל: the custom is justified, so let Klal Yisrael follow it.[15]

VI. The Haftarah — Where Does It Come From?

On Shabbos we read seven עליות, as discussed when the series covered Monday and Thursday, and then a הפטרה. Where does it come from, and why do we read it? "The וועלט says הפטורה; it's wrong. It's הפטרה: מפטיר, הפטרה. It's a big mistake people make... you're not going to go to גיהנום for that." But the question stands, and there is a "very radical מחלוקת, extreme from one to the other, between the ראשונים." Again, "this is all Jewish history."

First view: Rashi in Sefer HaPardes — a daily Haftarah

רש"י, in ספר הפרדס (not רש"י על הש"ס or על התורה, but a sefer that quotes much from him), says that in earlier times, every single day, not only on Monday and Thursday, the custom was that after davening everyone sat down and read a chapter of נביא. "You ever knew this? Every day after Shacharis, you close the סידור..." Why? Because we want people, before they go to work, to have read מקרא (חומש), נביא, משנה, ברייתא and גמרא.[16]

The Rav walks through how we already fulfil each. מקרא: קריאת שמע. The Gemara in מנחות (the Rav says daf צ; it is 99b) teaches that one who recites קריאת שמע morning and evening has fulfilled והגית בו יומם ולילה, but it is forbidden to say this before עמי הארצות, because they will conclude that they need do nothing more ("ברוך השם we're a group of תלמידי חכמים, so it's okay to share this").[17] משנה: איזהו מקומן ("What if you don't say it? You're in big trouble"), and אלו דברים after ברכת התורה ("הלוואי people should say it"). ברייתא: רבי ישמעאל אומר and others throughout davening. "But when do we say נביא?" A congregant points to ובא לציון גואל.

So the ancient custom, per Rashi, was to read a chapter of נביא every day. "You happily would be בקיאים in תנ"ך if we would do this every day." The Rav suspects Rashi is quoting a custom from before the Gemara was even written, which would explain why he lists מקרא, משנה, ברייתא, תלמוד together.

Why did it stop? The reason is socioeconomic. רש"י writes רבתה העניות, and the Rav finds it "sad to read it": people were poor and needed to make a living. You cannot hold them in shul for an extra half hour for a סדר; "you can invite them to a דף, but to force them to say it's part of davening, don't take your תפילין off until you read a פרשה in נביא, that we can't do anymore." So the custom was nullified except on Shabbos, when nobody is rushing to work. "If anything, they're going to the קידוש club, but they can come back to hear the הפטרה, and they shouldn't go to the קידוש club in the first place!"

Hence, according to Rashi, the הפטרה is a דין of every day, with "nothing to do with" the weekly Torah portion, and we keep it only on Shabbos. This is "a חמור'דיגע חידוש, people don't know this." There are ברכות before and after, as part of Rashi's שיטה. In those days the reading did not have to be מעין המאורע; it was just a chapter of תנ"ך. They may have started from יהושע and read a chapter a day, "I don't know if they read two chapters a day... we don't have a clear indication as to what they read."

Haftaros that match the day — the chasan, the Rambam, and Rav Sherira Gaon

Over time the הפטרה became connected to the week. Even now on ימים טובים, ראש חודש, or a Shabbos that is ערב ראש חודש, we read a haftarah that fits the day rather than the portion. And the רמ"א brings a custom regarding a חתן: when there was a חתן in shul on Shabbos אויפרוף, they read a different haftarah, שוש אשיש, a chapter in ישעיה that speaks of a חתן and כלה, one of the שבע דנחמתא. The Sefardim adopted it, and "we don't do it." (A participant asks what would happen in a big Sefardic shul with a חתן every week; another suggests חיי שרה, the portion of שידוכים. The Rav admits he did not know of the custom and keeps going.)[18]

"But let me tell you an even bigger חידוש, and I guarantee you no one knows it. I only found it out this morning." The ראשונים write that when something significant happened that week, they did not read the haftarah of the week but the haftarah relating to the event. When the רמב"ם passed away, throughout Eretz Yisrael they read that Shabbos a chapter in שמואל (the Rav forgets the exact date of his יארצייט, "somewhere in שבט"), and it happens to be the chapter learned in the Thursday night shiur two weeks ago: the ark of the covenant being taken from Klal Yisrael by the Philistines. The ark in exile; the Rambam was "like the ארון הברית, the כח התורה of his generation"; his נשמה went up to שמים. When רב שרירא גאון died, in the Geonic era, they read a special chapter in תנ"ך about the death of a צדיק. "We don't do that today, we don't even do what [the Sefardim do] when there's a חתן."[19]

That is Rashi's view in sum: the haftarah became connected to that Shabbos, but it was originally a custom for every day.

VII. The Second View — The Avudraham, the Greek Decree, and Sefer HaTishbi

The second שיטה is that of the אבודרהם, who brings (and this, too, is Jewish history) that once there was a גזירה not to read from the Torah, not to read the פרשה. חז"ל, or whoever was there at the time, feared that people would forget about קריאת התורה: they would go to shul, daven Shacharis, "no break," Musaf, and go home. So they instituted a תקנה: since we cannot read from the Torah, read at least twenty-one verses from the Prophets, and someone gets up and makes a ברכה. Twenty-one because Shabbos has seven עליות and each needs at least three verses (3 × 7). The haftarah is to be on the theme of what would have been read had it not been for the decree, that Shabbos's פרשה, so that "as if you read in the Torah, at least people won't forget about reading." When the decree was lifted, they went back to reading the Torah and the haftarah remained.[20]

According to the אבודרהם, then, there was "never a concept of reading הפטרה every day like Rashi" (a "major מחלוקת in Jewish history"). What about reading נביא daily? The congregant's point stands: ובא לציון גואל is a verse from נביא, and a few other verses from the Prophets are said in the davening, so we fulfil the obligation of ללמוד נביא every day through those. But since the haftarah was a response to the decree, the question is when it happened: "Give us some more historical context. When was there a decree that you could daven but not read from the Torah, though you could read from the Prophets?"

There is a sefer, "and this we only discovered in the 1500s," called ספר התשבי, which the Rav loves, "a very rare sefer." The author is much better known than the book: רב אליהו בחור, or Elijah Levita, "more known by Christians than Jews," born in Germany and living in Italy in the 1500s, "a brilliant man," a grammarian, one of the most prolific writers of דקדוק of his day. (The Rav stops to ask what a grammarian is: "someone who's an expert in language, in grammar.")[21]

Elijah Levita and the Christian Hebraists

He taught כלל ישראל, and more importantly he taught the early Christian Hebraists. In the 1500s there were many priests and, more important, cardinals who got into the Hebrew language; they studied the Bible, משנה, תוספתא, גמרא, and some became real תלמידי חכמים, "להבדיל... They wore a cross, but they were big תלמידי חכמים." They were "immensely curious" about our literature and took a lot from it, and many Christian theologians afterward took חז"ל and dressed it in a Christian garb, להבדיל. "A lot of it comes from Eliyahu Bachur."

Eliyahu Bachur decided it was his job to teach the Christians the truth ("today he would probably be put in חרם"). He even boarded with one of the main cardinals of his day, whose name the Rav cannot recall ("there was a famous cardinal; you go to a גוי, he'll know"). He lived with him, unmarried (בחור), edited his books, and taught him and the other Christian Hebraists about all of our literature. The Rav then remembers a name: Sebastian Münster, "one of their גדולים," who "learned Torah from Eliyahu Bachur."

A question from the room: may one teach Torah to non-Jews? A congregant asks. The Rav: "You're not allowed to teach גוים Torah. A גוי that learns Torah is חייב מיתה, it's a terrible thing." He recalls a famous responsum of רב משה פיינשטיין in אגרות משה: a rabbi gave a daily shiur and a non-Jewish woman, an educated and intellectually curious one, began coming. The מגיד שיעור felt very uncomfortable: should he throw her out, with the חילול השם? רב משה ruled that when one teaches the Jewish public collectively and a non-Jew happens to walk in, there is no איסור; he was מקיל. Another congregant raises the Rambam's ולא שבט לוי בלבד אלא כל איש ואיש, wondering whether that includes non-Jews; the Rav answers that it does not and cites סנהדרין דף נ"ט. As for Eliyahu Bachur, the Rav suggests he was probably frowned upon in his day for teaching non-Jews, "maybe bad things happened as a result of it, it's very hard to tell": these people did not convert, "they became even bigger Christians." And a lot of what Eliyahu Bachur wrote, later אחרונים were not sure could be trusted, but it did enter our literature and the פוסקים.[22]

The Greek decree, the Levush, and why never Kesuvim

Eliyahu Bachur writes that it was מלכות יון, in the days of אנטיוכוס, who ruled Eretz Yisrael, who made the גזירה against reading the Torah every Shabbos. The לבוש adds, in the laws of Shabbos רפ"א (the Rav's citation), something that "is very מבהיל, I don't understand it": the decree was not to read Torah and not to read כתובים, only נביאים. "Maybe the Greeks thought the Prophets were okay to read, but the כתובים no." And that, says the Levush, is why we never take a haftarah from כתובים: "It's a Gemara in מגילה, but nobody knows why. Says the לבוש, I'll tell you why. It's all history." Since then, writes Eliyahu Bachur, they read from נביא every week, and the תקנה remained.

VIII. Questions From the Room — Three Years, Seven Aliyos, and Machar Chodesh

The triennial cycle and the Cairo shul

A congregant asked a question that the אחרונים discuss: what about the מנהג בני בבל, who read the Torah over three years, with much shorter פרשיות? The אחרונים point out that, according to them, whatever small portion was read that week, the haftarah was chosen to match it, so there were many more haftaros than we have today (a participant adds that they also had a different division of the חומש). The Rav then brings the great traveler ר' בנימין מטודלה, Benjamin of Tudela, "I always like to quote him": he writes that he went to Cairo, and "right next door to the Rambam's shul there was another shul," where they read according to the ancient custom, completing the Torah only once every three years, and the haftarah in each week was whatever connected to that particular portion.[23]

Why seven aliyos?

A participant asks about the Gemara in מגילה דף כ"ג, which gives several reasons for the seven עליות, one of which is the seven שרים at the beginning of מגילת אסתר; "a very mystical piece of Gemara nobody really understands." Then a חידוש הפלא ופלא from one of the אחרונים: some people in earlier times came to shul only on Shabbos. Even in Europe, those who lived in small villages with no מנין would go to the city only on Shabbos, sometimes also on Monday and Thursday. They had thus missed, that week, six (the Rav first says seven) recitations of ברכו את ה' המבורך; so, to make up for the ברכו's, we have seven עליות, each עולה making a ברכה. "I think the לבוש brings it. The ראשונים don't say it, but that's to answer your question."[24]

"Machar chodesh"

Another congregant asks: what about a haftarah like מחר חודש, which has nothing to do with the day and is about tomorrow? The Rav calls it an excellent question and says the מגן אברהם raises it. According to many מנהגים, you read מחר חודש only when Shabbos is ערב ראש חודש (Sunday being Rosh Chodesh), and when Shabbos is itself Rosh Chodesh, "not like our מנהג," we read the haftarah of ראש חודש. "But it's a good question. I don't have a good answer; the מגן אברהם doesn't like it."[25]

The Rav closes by saying that, אם ירצה השם, next week the shiur will take up the ברכות of the haftarah and the question of whether one should read the haftarah together with the בעל קורא or not. The shiur ends with the concluding words רצה הקדוש ברוך הוא לזכות את ישראל, לפיכך הרבה להם תורה ומצות, שנאמר: ה' חפץ למען צדקו, יגדיל תורה ויאדיר.
