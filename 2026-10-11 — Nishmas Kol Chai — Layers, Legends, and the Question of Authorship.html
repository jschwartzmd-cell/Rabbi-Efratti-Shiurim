Nishmas Kol Chai (נשמת כל חי) — Layers, Legends, and the Question of Authorship

From נשמת to שוכן עד — Birchas HaShir and the rain-prayer, Reb Kloynimus Ba'al HaNes, the יצחק/רבקה acrostic, שמעון בן שטח and שמעון כיפא, and the Chasidic fire of Nishmas
Recorded 11 October 2026 · 30 Tishrei 5787 (Rosh Chodesh) · Reworked from the original שיעור

Back on the arc of the Shabbos tefillos after the Days of Awe, the Rav stands at the end of Pesukei DeZimra and asks the questions that have long fascinated him about נשמת כל חי: why it is so central that the Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה) requires a latecomer to say it, who composed it, when it entered the seder, and why on Shabbos. He peels back its layers: ברכת השיר on the fourth cup (Pesachim), the rain-prayer ואילו פינו (Berachos), the lines אלהי הראשונים והאחרונים that the Chida (החיד"א) traces to the miracle of Reb Kloynimus Ba'al HaNes in Jerusalem, and a late section hiding the acrostic of יצחק and רבקה. Then come the wild authorship claims: the Rokeach (הרוקח) on its supreme antiquity, Reb Hertz Treves's backwards acrostic שמעון and the legend of שמעון כיפא (Peter), which the Rav says other Rishonim tell and Reb Hertz quotes, and which the Machzor Vitry (מחזור ויטרי) so fiercely rejects (Sefer Chasidim (ספר חסידים) also calls him פטר חמור, though it says he began as a tzaddik). He closes with reasons for Shabbos, the Chasidic experience of Nishmas, and the unresolved mystery of שוכן עד.

I. Back on Track — Nishmas as One Long Tefillah

After many weeks of Rosh HaShanah and Yom Kippur davening, the Rav explains, we are back on the arc of the Shabbos tefillos. The last time the shiur worked on this track was seven or eight weeks ago, when it learned Pesukei DeZimra (פסוקי דזמרה); we now stand at the very end of Pesukei DeZimra. This is a subject that has always fascinated the Rav, and he shares what he found. (The shiur was dedicated לעילוי נשמת. The Rav gave the dedication in two stages. First, in passing, he thanked Reb Meir Weissman, who sponsored the shiur and was not there that morning because he had gone to learn with his grandson in Darchei Torah, and mentioned, without having the names in front of him, his wife's father and grandfather: די נשמה זאל האבן אן עליה, "and for all Jews a yeshuah." Then, after the coffee break, he restated it: the shiur, and all the berachos and Torah learned, are לעילוי נשמת ר׳ יוסי גבאי טהרה (apparently a further dedicatee; the Rav's wording is not entirely clear, so this is for the Rav to confirm), and לעילוי נשמת her father, דוד בן ר' משה (Danny Kriegsman, "I'm sure many of you knew him"), and her grandfather, יואל בן מרדכי הלוי, די נשמה זאל האבן אן עליה. All names, and the spelling Weissman/Weisman, are to be confirmed with the Rav before printing.)

At the end of Pesukei DeZimra on Shabbos we say נִשְׁמַת כָּל חַי. It is a long and very unusual tefillah. In our siddurim it is printed in separate paragraphs, but the Rav asks us to notice what it is: one continuous tefillah, running from נשמת all the way to שׁוֹכֵן עַד and then straight into וּבְכֵן יִשְׁתַּבַּח שִׁמְךָ לָעַד מַלְכֵּנוּ. It is a long introduction to Yishtabach (ישתבח), the berachah that closes Pesukei DeZimra. Just as Baruch SheAmar (ברוך שאמר) opens Pesukei DeZimra, Yishtabach closes it, so that Pesukei DeZimra are sandwiched between two berachos, and Nishmas is the great crescendo leading into the second.

נִשְׁמַת כָּל חַי תְּבָרֵךְ אֶת שִׁמְךָ ה' אֱלֹהֵינוּ, וְרוּחַ כָּל בָּשָׂר תְּפָאֵר וּתְרוֹמֵם זִכְרְךָ מַלְכֵּנוּ תָּמִיד

The Rav sets out four questions for the morning:

  • Why do we add Nishmas on Shabbos?
  • Who composed this tefillah?
  • When did it enter the seder?
  • Why does Klal Yisrael take it so seriously?

He is candid that he is "a little confused myself as to the timeline." The aim is to work it out together from the Rishonim and Geonim who quote parts of Nishmas, and to arrive at some picture of how it was written, why, and when it came into the seder.

II. Why Klal Yisrael Takes Nishmas So Seriously

Before the history, the Rav gives the halachic proof of how seriously Nishmas is treated. The Mishnah Berurah (משנה ברורה), in the name of the Acharonim, rules on the Jew who arrives late to shul on Shabbos morning. ("First of all, why do you come late to shul on Shabbos morning?") He may begin davening with the tzibbur, but certain pieces of Pesukei DeZimra are mandatory before he does, before he goes on to Yotzer Or.[1] Two of them are Baruch SheAmar and Yishtabach, the two berachos that frame Pesukei DeZimra. Besides those two, he must say Ashrei and Nishmas.

The Rav says the Mishnah Berurah goes further. If you skipped Nishmas because there was no time and you wanted to catch a proper tefillah with the tzibbur, make sure to say Nishmas after davening. He does not tell you to make up all the other Pesukei DeZimra afterwards, and he does not require you to make up Shirat HaYam (שירת הים). (Many poskim consider Shirat HaYam very important too, and if you have time you say it as well.) But Nishmas must be made up. The Rav calls this the true measure of its status: Nishmas has become part of the core structure of Pesukei DeZimra, specifically on Shabbos and Yom Tov.

"What is so special about Nishmas?" That is the question the rest of the shiur is built around.

III. The First Layer — Birchas HaShir on the Fourth Cup

The first mention of Nishmas in Chazal is in the Gemara in Pesachim, near the end of the masechta, on daf 118a (the Rav: "Daf Kuf Yud Ches, amud alef"). On Seder night the Gemara goes through what is done on each of the four cups. A cup requires a berachah and praise of Hashem, so what is said over the fourth cup? The Gemara says that, according to one opinion, one recites Birchas HaShir (ברכת השיר), "the blessing over song," and asks what exactly that is.[2] The answer is Nishmas Kol Chai.

The Rav adds a precision. The Gemara does not mean the whole of today's Nishmas, only the first part, up to a certain point. There are different girsaos (גרסאות) as to where it ends, and some say it extends until וְאִלּוּ פִינוּ מָלֵא שִׁירָה כַּיָּם. The Gemara says nothing about Shabbos. It speaks of one night a year, the Seder, when one says this special tefillah.

The significance is chronological. If this part of Nishmas appears in the Gemara, it was evidently authored in the times of Chazal. As the Rav puts it, most Rishonim understand the answer to who composed it to be the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah, and specifically Ezra HaSofer: Ezra wrote this beautiful tefillah, and Chazal then decided it should be said on Seder night as Birchas HaShir. The second stretch, from ואילו פינו מלא שירה כים to the end of Nishmas, is also mentioned in the Gemara, and the Rav remarks how interesting it is that these two different things were put together.

IV. The Second Layer — וְאִלּוּ פִינוּ and the Prayer for Rain

From וְאִלּוּ פִינוּ מָלֵא שִׁירָה כַּיָּם onward, the Rav says, we are dealing with a different source. This is the dramatic, expansive passage in which we say that even if our mouths were as full of song as the sea, we could never thank Hashem enough:

וְאִלּוּ פִינוּ מָלֵא שִׁירָה כַּיָּם

This section is also in the Gemara, in Berachos, "daf Nun Tes," in the sugya on heavy rain.[3] When it rains in abundance (the Rav notes, "welcome to Mar-Cheshvan, the rain season") one makes a special berachah and tefillah of thanks. This is part of that prayer, and the Rav quotes its continuation, וְלִשׁוֹנֵנוּ רִנָּה כַּהֲמוֹן גַּלָּיו. We do not say it today for rain, and in practice nobody does, yet it appears in the Shulchan Aruch (the Rav: "it's a whole siman"; he gave no number). In the time of Chazal significant rain mattered enormously in the Middle East, for the crops and for agriculture, so a special prayer of gratitude was said.

Then someone took that passage out of the rain-prayers and fitted it together, "like a puzzle," with the Nishmas of the fourth cup on Seder night. They became one tefillah, and Klal Yisrael decided to say it every Shabbos. The Rav stresses how remarkable this is: two distinct texts from two different Talmudic contexts, welded into one.

V. The Third Layer — אֱלֹהֵי הָרִאשׁוֹנִים and Reb Kloynimus Ba'al HaNes

Now the Rav arrives at the part he finds most surprising. Within Nishmas, before וְאִלּוּ פִינוּ, we describe the greatness of Hashem:

אֱלֹהֵי הָרִאשׁוֹנִים וְהָאַחֲרוֹנִים, אֱלוֹהַּ כָּל בְּרִיּוֹת, אֲדוֹן כָּל תּוֹלָדוֹת ... וַה' לֹא יָנוּם וְלֹא יִישָׁן
הַמְעוֹרֵר יְשֵׁנִים, וְהַמֵּקִיץ נִרְדָּמִים, וְהַמֵּשִׂיחַ אִלְּמִים, וְהַמַּתִּיר אֲסוּרִים, וְהַסּוֹמֵךְ נוֹפְלִים, וְהַזּוֹקֵף כְּפוּפִים, וְהַמְפַעְנֵחַ נֶעֱלָמִים, וּלְךָ לְבַדְּךָ אֲנַחְנוּ מוֹדִים

Hashem "awakens the sleeping," "causes the mute to speak" (הַמֵּשִׂיחַ אִלְּמִים), and "deciphers the hidden" (הַמְפַעְנֵחַ נֶעֱלָמִים).

The Chida's Revelation

The Chida (החיד"א) wrote about a hundred sefarim, and one of them is a commentary on the Haggadah called Simchas HaRegel (שמחת הרגל). In it, at Nishmas in the Haggadah, he tells us something Klal Yisrael had never known.[4] This section of Nishmas, the lines describing Hashem who awakens the sleeping, opens the mouth of the mute and deciphers the hidden, was added only some 700 to 800 years ago, and the Chida tells us the story behind it. We say it every Shabbos.

The Man and His Title

In Jerusalem, the Rav relates, there lived a Jew named Reb Kloynimus (רב קלונימוס), later known as Ba'al HaNes (בעל הנס). He was the rav of Jerusalem at the time. The Rav places him there "700 years ago, approximately," but stresses that we have no exact date and no exact biography. "It's a shtikel of a mystery who this person was." Very few people in our history carry the title Ba'al HaNes. (There is a debate about who the original one was, whether the Tanna Rabbi Meir or someone later.) A person earns the title because of a miracle done through him or for him. At the time of the story he was not yet called Ba'al HaNes. He received the title only afterward.

The Blood Libel in Jerusalem

The setting is a classic blood libel, but in Eretz Yisrael. A Muslim child died, around Pesach time. The Rav points out that blood libels were not only a Christian phenomenon. This one took place in Eretz Yisrael, which the Rav, asking the room whose rule it was under, answers was the Turks, the Ottoman Empire, whose governor was called a Pasha (see note 5 on the Rav's chronology). (The Rav mentions a professor in Eretz Yisrael who published an academic work cataloguing all recorded blood libels. He could not recall the professor's name or whether this case appeared in the book. Most blood libels, he notes, happened in Christian countries.) The child's body was thrown into the courtyard of the shul, and the accusers came with the old lie: the Jews had slaughtered this child because they needed his blood to bake matzos.

Reb Kloynimus was the rav in Jerusalem at the time. He was told that the Pasha ("the pasha was the king") had resolved to put an end to the Jews and drive them out of Jerusalem because of the affair. The community's fate hung on this.

The Miracle

Reb Kloynimus went to the dead child. By means of the Divine Name (the Rav's phrasing is loose: "he said what he said," and later "putting the Shem HaKadosh on his mouth, by writing it or whatever"), the child, who had been dead for days, opened his mouth and said exactly who had killed him. A non-Jew had killed the child, and the child said who he was and where he was to be found. They went and found the man, and the Jewish community of Jerusalem was saved. This is why Reb Kloynimus is called Ba'al HaNes.

The Rav gives his own stance. Whatever the Chida writes sounds like a legend and a myth, and "you don't have to believe the story if you don't subscribe to the Chida. I believe it. I have no problem." He adds that the story is not the Chida's alone. Reb Yaakov Yisrael Algazi (רב יעקב ישראל אלגאזי; the Rav first said "Reb Yisrael" and then corrected himself), whom the Rav calls "the Maharit Algazi's son," one of the gedolim of Yerushalayim who came originally from Izmir, Turkey, also records it. The Chida does not quote Algazi, and Algazi does not quote the Chida, so it was evidently a story that people knew.[5]

A Detail Worth Noticing — It Happened on Shabbos

The miracle took place on Shabbos. To save the community, Reb Kloynimus had to be mechalel Shabbos (מחלל שבת) for pikuach nefesh (פיקוח נפש); the Rav says "he had to write a שם, something." In the Rav's words: "He did חילול שבת for פיקוח נפש, but he held he's not on the same מדרגה." This is the thread that ties the story to our Shabbos tefillah.

Burial and the Lost Gravesite

Because of that one act of chilul Shabbos, Reb Kloynimus asked the community to bury him outside Har HaZeisim (הר הזיתים), not in the regular beis hachaim on Har HaZeisim where the Jewish community members were buried at the time. His reasoning was: "I don't deserve to be buried amongst Klal Yisrael." The Rav emphasizes that this was a great tzaddik, who held himself accountable for a single instance of chilul Shabbos.

His kever was well known. A pile of stones lay on top of it, and there was a famous segulah (סגולה) in Yerushalayim before 1948. Someone in need of a yeshuah (ישועה) would go daven at the kever and take a stone with him for whatever he needed, and he would swear (שבועה) to bring the stone back and cover the kever again. In 1948, under Jordanian rule, the place was lost. We know approximately where it was, but no one knows exactly, so people no longer go. The Rav calls it "another one of these kivrei tzaddikim stories."

From the Miracle to the Text

After the incident, according to the Chida's account, Reb Kloynimus wrote a new section of tefillah and incorporated it into Nishmas, the very lines above. The Rav spells out how each one fits the story:

  • הַמֵּשִׂיחַ אִלְּמִים — Hashem opens the mouth of those who cannot speak. In the story He opened the mouth of the dead child, and that saved the community.
  • הַמְפַעְנֵחַ נֶעֱלָמִים — Hashem "deciphers the hidden." Who killed the child was hidden, and it was brought to light.
  • The framing lines — Hashem who protects all of Israel, even in the midst of so terrible a libel.

And since the miracle occurred on Shabbos, he wrote it into the tefillah of Shabbos. The Rav's instruction: next time you say Nishmas, have this in mind. "Can you believe this stuff? The first time I saw this I thought, come on. But that's what the Acharonim write." So all of Klal Yisrael commemorates an obscure miracle that happened, in the Rav's words here, "a couple of centuries ago" in Yerushalayim (earlier he said about 700 years ago; see note 5), because Reb Kloynimus Ba'al HaNes wrote it.

The Proof from the Gaonic Siddurim

The Rav gives evidence that this section is a later addition. If you look in the siddurim of the Geonim, Rav Amram Gaon (רב עמרם גאון) and Rav Saadia Gaon (רב סעדיה גאון), roughly a thousand years ago, this part of Nishmas does not appear.[6] So it was obviously added later. Whether it was added because of the Kloynimus story, the Rav leaves open ("Now, whether it happened because of the story of Rav Kloynimus, huh?"); that is the Chida's account.

The nusach (נוסח) differences bear on this. As the Rav puts it, Ashkenaz does not say some of it ("some of it they don't say, but they have the essential piece they do have"), and he asks, with a wry aside, why one davens Nusach Ashkenaz. But in Nusach Sefard, he says, "we say the whole thing, משיח אלמים, מפענח נעלמים." He does not specify which lines Ashkenaz leaves out.[7] "But the Geonim already have this tefillah of Nishmas itself," the Rav notes.

VI. A Much Later Section (the Rav's Claim) — From וְכָל הַלְּבָבוֹת to שׁוֹכֵן עַד

The Rav then turns to the end of Nishmas. The passage that began with ואילו פינו, the Rav says, is the shevach Chazal wrote on the rain berachah, and it ends with וְכָל לָשׁוֹן לְךָ תִשָּׁבַע, וְכָל קוֹמָה לְפָנֶיךָ תִשְׁתַּחֲוֶה. Based on his own research in ancient siddurim, the section that follows, beginning with וְכָל הַלְּבָבוֹת יִירָאוּךָ and running all the way through שׁוֹכֵן עַד (the Rav: "until Yishtabach"), is a later part of the tefillah and leads into Yishtabach. In his words, it "was authored much, much later." Who authored it is, as he says, a big debate, and this is where hints to the author appear in the text itself.

The Acrostic — Yitzchak and Rivka

The Rav asks the room to open a siddur to the line beginning בְּפִי יְשָׁרִים. Many siddurim, including the ArtScroll (the Rav asks whether they have the ראשי תיבות of יצחק and רבקה in bold), print certain letters in bold:

בְּפִי ישָׁרִים תִּתְרוֹמָם, וּבְשִׂפְתֵי צדִּיקִים תִּתְבָּרַךְ, וּבִלְשׁוֹן חסִידִים תִּתְקַדָּשׁ, וּבְקֶרֶב קדוֹשִׁים תִּתְהַלָּל

The first letters of ישרים, צדיקים, חסידים, קדושים are yud, tzadi, chet, kuf, spelling "Yitzchak" (יצחק). And if you take the third letter of each of the four verbs, תתרומם, תתברך, תתקדש, תתהלל, you get resh, beis, kuf, heh: "Rivka" (רבקה).

Some say, the Rav reports, that this section was said by none other than Yitzchak and Rivka, as in the beginning of Parashas Toldos (וַיֶּעְתַּר יִצְחָק לַה' לְנֹכַח אִשְׁתּוֹ).[8] Rivka was barren (עקרה) and they finally had a child (twins), and on that day they said this tefillah to commemorate the occasion. According to that view, the husband and wife wrote this tefillah together.

The Machzor Vitry's Objection

The Machzor Vitry (מחזור ויטרי) quotes this and objects. If it was Yitzchak and Rivka, he says, why would their tefillah have been incorporated into Shacharis? Yitzchak instituted Minchah.[9] If it were their tefillah it should be in Minchah. "Something doesn't add up." The Machzor Vitry, as the Rav reports it, concludes that it must be a later couple, another Yitzchak and Rivka, who for some reason thanked Hashem and composed a tefillah. For reasons unknown to us Klal Yisrael adopted the tefillah, and it sits at the latter part of Nishmas. (The Rav: "Next time you have a yeshuah with your wife, write a tefillah together.") "That's what they used to do." It remains, he says, a mystery.

VII. Who Wrote Nishmas? — The Rokeach's Claim

The Rav returns to the beginning of Nishmas and what fascinates him most: no other tefillah has such extreme opinions as to who wrote it. Some Rishonim say the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah. (The Rav said "most Rishonim" in Section III and "some Rishonim" here; both are his wording and are left as he said them. See note 2.) But the most extreme claim is in the Rokeach (הרוקח), Rabbeinu Elazar of Worms, a Rishon the shiur has discussed at length in earlier shiurim on tefillah (the Rav recalls that "the whole story of the Rokeach and the Ramban" was told there).[10]

The Rokeach holds that Nishmas is the most ancient tefillah in existence, the first tefillah ever authored. It was written and recited even before Rebbi (Rabbi Yehudah HaNasi) redacted the Mishnayos, so it comes before the Oral Torah was written down. (The Rav: "We don't know that about any other tefillah.") He draws a further conclusion from the lines discussed above (which, the Rav notes, "doesn't fit" with what the Chida said). When we say that Hashem is matir asurim, the one who awakens the sleeping, opens the mouth of the mute, supports the fallen, and straightens the bent, what does that remind us of? Birchos HaShachar. (The Rav notes that the shiur spoke about Birchos HaShachar on Shabbos morning at Chumash Rashi, and that it is a Gemara in Berachos, "daf Samech, Samech Alef.")[11]

The Rokeach's chiddush (חידוש) is this. In the olden days, before Chazal composed Birchos HaShachar in the Gemara, there were no Birchos HaShachar. People said this tefillah of Hamei'ir Yesheinim instead. It was how they expressed thanks: I woke up, I opened my mouth, I stood up. "I never heard this from anywhere else," the Rav says. The Rav adds that this is why, by the way, people are so drawn to Nishmas: all over Eretz Yisrael they go to the Kosel for forty days in a row and say Nishmas, and get a shidduch. It is a new thing; the mekubalim say to do it. (More on this below.)[12]

VIII. Another Candidate — Shimon ben Shetach and the Reverse Acrostic

Now the Rav moves to a story he calls "an urban myth, but it has sources." One of the very first siddurim printed in the history of printing was the siddur of Reb Naftali Hertz Treves (ר' נפתלי הירץ טריוויש), known as "Reb Hertz Shatz" (ש"ץ, an acronym for shliach tzibbur, since he was a chazzan who knew the tefillos). In an age of handwritten manuscripts, he printed a siddur. He was born in Worms, buried in Frankfurt, and lived in the 1500s. (The Rav says the group did not visit his grave in Frankfurt; "we should go back.") His siddur is quoted very often by the Magen Avraham (מגן אברהם), the Shelah and other Acharonim, so it is an important sefer.[13]

By Nishmas he cites a yesh omrim ("some say") and asks: do you know who wrote it? The answer is far earlier than the Anshei Knesses HaGedolah or the Geonim, and it would explain why the Rokeach holds that it predates Birchos HaShachar. He says Nishmas was written by Shimon ben Shetach (שמעון בן שטח).

Who Was Shimon ben Shetach?

The Rav reminds the room. Shimon ben Shetach lived in the time of Alexander Yannai (ינאי המלך, Alexander Jannaeus), and he was the brother of the queen, Shlomtzion, who was married to Yannai. The Rav says "the whole story with Yeshke began with Shimon ben Shetach; his talmid is Rabbi Yehoshua ben Perachya," and that Shimon ben Shetach was the rebbi of Shemaya and Avtalyon. He belongs to the era of the Zugos, "the beginning of Pirkei Avos." This is long before the Tannaim, among the early Chachmei Yisrael.[14] So if Reb Hertz is right, Nishmas is extremely early. The Rav quips that this "explains why everybody gets so excited about it."

The Proof — Reading the Paragraphs Backwards

How does Reb Hertz know? Look at the first letters of the paragraphs of Nishmas, in reverse order, working from the end of the tefillah:

  • Shin — שַׁוְעַת עֲנִיִּים אַתָּה תִשְׁמַע, the last of the sections.
  • Mem — מִי יִדְמֶה לָּךְ. ("I wrote it down for myself because I knew I'd forget it.")
  • Ayin — עַד הֵנָּה עֲזָרוּנוּ רַחֲמֶיךָ.
  • Vav — וְאִלּוּ פִינוּ מָלֵא שִׁירָה כַּיָּם.
  • Nun — נִשְׁמַת.

Read backwards, shin, mem, ayin, vav, nun spell "Shimon" (שמעון), written into the tefillah in reverse. Reb Hertz concludes that it was Shimon ben Shetach. The Rav is enthusiastic: "I don't know of any other tefillah in the seder that was written by such an early source." But then, he says, Reb Hertz says "the most controversial thing that was ever said about anything in our seder."

IX. The Other Shimon — Shimon Kipa and the Infiltration

The Rav now tells the legend behind that controversy: "I know you know parts of this legend, but I want to expound upon it a little bit because I think it's fascinating and very important." He says openly that some Rishonim are very upset when it is told: "I'm going to say it because other Rishonim do say it; Reb Hertz quotes it." In the legend the "Shimon" is Shimon Kipa (שמעון כיפא), "the true translation is Shimon Kefa," who is none other than Peter, Saint Peter of the Vatican.[15]

The World of the Early Followers

The Rav sets the scene. In the early days of Christianity, Yeshke (ישקע, ימח שמו וזכרו) was a Jew, and his disciples were all Jews. The Rav says: "We're learning Torah here, but I'm saying it for a purpose, להבדיל אלף אלפי הבדלות." He points to the famous painting of the Last Supper: that supper, he says, was a Seder night. These Jews were part and parcel of the community (the Rav notes that the shiur has spoken about this several times). They davened in shul with us, sat beside us, and believed in Yeshke. For Chazal there was a real problem: how do you make a distinction between those who believe and those who do not, when they all sit together in the same shul?

The Double Agent

The legend, the Rav says, "reads like a novel." The Chachmei Yisrael decided, in the days of Bayis Sheni, to infiltrate the cult, which was still very small at the time. They were persecuted: the Jews hated them and the Romans hated them, "as we know from the story of the cross." They would send a double agent, a God-fearing Jew who would make believe he subscribed to all their narratives and beliefs. Because he was a person of great stature, he would be able, once the founder was gone, to make new rules for the Christians, so that it would be recognizable who is a Christian and who is a Jew. The goal was to completely alienate the Christians from our congregation.

They chose Shimon Kipa, one of the Chachmei Yisrael of the time, a Tanna (the Rav does not know which). According to the story, which appears in a midrash whose source is debated, they brought him to the Beis HaMikdash itself and swore him in. They told him: take off your yarmulke (the Rav interrupts himself, "they didn't have yarmulkes back then"), make believe you are a Christian, believe in Yeshke, do whatever they do, and on one condition: make new rules that create the distinction. And "we will be mochel (מוחלים), we will take upon ourselves all the aveiros you may end up doing for the rest of your life." According to the midrashim, he infiltrated the upper ranks of the movement.

What Peter Did

In the Rav's account of the legend (and "this we know from the New Testament"), Peter is the one who:

  • Turned Shabbos into Sunday, so that "you keep Sunday, okay? You're the Christians."
  • Replaced the festivals: Pesach (the Last Supper) became Easter, Shavuos became Pentecost, and, the Rav continues, Sukkos became "Krazmach" (קראצמך, the Rav's Yiddish for Christmas) and Shemini Atzeres became New Year's.
  • Had a dream, in the New Testament (the Rav: "there has to be some part of this legend [that has] to be true"), in which, "this is all made up, obviously, according to our tradition," Yeshke (ימח שמו וזכרו) came to him and the laws of the Torah, specifically kashrus, were abolished: "we should only eat treif," and his disciples began to eat treif. This too is a way to make the distinction. (The Rav says he knows exactly where in the New Testament this is but will not say, "because you're going to think I'm crazy.")[16]

The Rav notes that many other things in the New Testament, and in our sources, point to this Peter. He was a Tanna, the Rav says, "and he was heilig," and he is the one who wrote Nishmas. (Here the Rav says "That's over here, Tashbetz, right?", apparently pointing to a source; see note 15.) The conclusion: "So Nishmas comes from Peter." And, the Rav says, that is unsettling.

The Fast of the Ninth of Teves

Reb David, a member of the shul whose "tremendous bekius" the Rav praises, adds a source: a gloss (hagah) of the Baruch Ta'am (ברוך טעם) at the Shulchan Aruch in Orach Chaim 580, the siman dealing with fast days that are no longer observed.[17] There the Shulchan Aruch records the fast of the ninth of Teves, the day before Asarah B'Teves, and says of it, in the Rav's paraphrase, ולא נודע למה, "nobody knows why we fast that day" (the wording on Sefaria is לא נודע איזה היא הצרה שאירע בו, that it is not known which calamity occurred on that day). The Rav notes that a phrase like this almost never appears elsewhere in the Shulchan Aruch, and that this is one of the fasts we no longer keep. Among the "many different opinions of the nosei keilim," the Rav mentions one that Ezra HaSofer died on that day, "like it says in the selichos of Asarah B'Teves." The Baruch Ta'am, the Rav explains, was Rabbi Baruch Frankel-Teomim, the Rav of Leipnik, "the father-in-law of the Tzanzer Rav, one of the great Acharonim." In his margin to the Shulchan Aruch he writes something very mysterious: מצאתי כתב יד, "I found a manuscript" saying that this is the day on which Shimon HaKipa passed away. We commemorate it by fasting because of the great service he did for Klal Yisrael by making the distinction between Jews and Christians.

The Rav says that this adds to the legend: there was a person Shimon Kipa, and he wrote Nishmas. "That's why שמעון, not שמעון בן שטח": he put his initials in backwards, as noted above (Shin from שועת עניים, Mem from מי ידמה לך, Ayin from עד הנה עזרונו, Vav from ואילו פינו, and Nun, נשמת), "Shimon, hence Shimon Kipa." Then the Rav adds: "Now, so maybe Shimon Kipa was a Nusach Sefard guy. I hear what you're saying, I hear what you're saying."

Machzor Vitry's Fury, and Sefer Chasidim

The Machzor Vitry (מחזור ויטרי), by Rabbi Simcha of Vitry, a student of Rashi (the Rav calls him Rashi's mechutan), was furious.[18] The Rav paraphrases the Machzor Vitry:

Whoever says over what I just said, when the Beis HaMikdash will be rebuilt, he will have to bring a korban chatas. He is not allowed to say such a thing. It is not possible, חס ושלום, that Nishmas was written by Peter. And he calls him הַנָּבָל פֶּטֶר חָמוֹר, "the scoundrel, Peter the donkey."

He was a Jew, the Rav says, as they all were: "Peter, obviously, was a Jew, but he was a כופר." The Sefer Chasidim (ספר חסידים), the Rav adds, also calls him "Peter Chamor," although it says he began as a tzaddik.[19] "So nobody really knows what the story with this Peter is."

Why "Peter"? Why "Kefa"?

The Rav adds the explanations for the names. His name was originally Shimon, and the New Testament gives his father's name as Bar Yonah. According to some sources, he was called Peter because in Lashon Kodesh peter means to exempt (פוטר): he exempted the Christians, who had been Jews, from the mitzvos. He told them, in effect, "you can eat treif, you can be mechalel Shabbos, do it on Sunday." (The Rav thanks a participant for reminding him of this.) So "a lot of things point to the story being true."

The name Kipa or Kefa is Aramaic for a rock (סלע), and Yeshke gave it to him. "From now on your name is going to be Peter, Patrus" (the Rav: "that's the name in ancient Latin"). In the New Testament it is said that "on this rock I will build my congregation."[20] Peter would be the rock that spreads the faith, the rock of all. The Rav adds that there was a major, lifelong machlokes between Peter and Paul. One of the big issues was whether Christianity is meant for gentiles at all, or only for Jews. Peter, the Rav says, held that it was mainly meant for Jews. For us, he adds, Peter was, according to the Baruch Ta'am, "a big tzaddik, a big Tanna." Catholics consider him the first pope, taken to Rome, where he died.

The Rav relates another midrash: at the end of his life, when he had had enough of the foolishness, Peter wanted to return to Yiddishkeit. "If it was for the sake of Heaven or not, we'll never know." He sat on a rock, in a tower on top of a rock somewhere in Rome (they claim to know where), which is another reason for the name Kefa. According to our tradition, he wrote many piyutim and tefillos, and one of them was Nishmas, which he sent to Chazal in Eretz Yisrael. They loved it, and they loved him, so much that they made every Jew say it every Shabbos.

The Seder Avodah of Yom Kippur

There is another composition attributed to him by the same legend. The Rav interjects that the machzor "says you're not allowed to say it" (apparently the claim of Peter's authorship, as above), and then names it: אַתָּה כוֹנַנְתָּ עוֹלָם מֵרֹאשׁ, the Seder Avodah of Musaf on Yom Kippur, the holiest day of the year.[21] (The Rav mentions that there are two nuschaos for the Avodah, Atah Konanta and Amitz Koach, Sefard and Ashkenaz: "one makes sense, one doesn't.") "He wrote Atah Konanta." The legend claims that this poetry too came originally from Shimon Kefa, and the Rav ties this to why we commemorate his yahrzeit on that day. "Again, big machlokes."

Some Asides from the Room

There was a back-and-forth with the shiur. Is it true that Peter is buried under St. Peter's Basilica? The Rav says Christians claim so, and notes a debate among archaeologists, with another church in Italy claiming to have his skull (someone even tried to sell "bones of Peter" on eBay). If he was one of the Tannaim, the Rav muses, it might be a holy (heilig) place, "so maybe you could go to St. Peter's Basilica, chas v'shalom." On whether to visit: someone had just asked the Rav the previous night, about to travel to Rome, and the Rav told him not to go, since it is a place of Christian worship (the Rav says the Pope does mass there twice a year). "That's what I hold; maybe others can hold otherwise. If you went there, do teshuvah." Ari asked why they did not rather infiltrate and slowly but surely try kiruv. The Rav answers (and calls it a good question): "I wasn't there, so I don't know," but it must have been very important to make that distinction. He adds a joke that he chose not to say: "Imagine if they had Chabad in the times of Yeshke."

X. Why Shabbos? Why Not Every Day?

The Rav now asks what he calls the "dafka Shabbos" question. Nishmas was incorporated into the seder specifically on Shabbos (and Yom Tov). Why? He gives several answers.

At this turn (the Rav: "Now we have to understand why on Shabbos. Yes, Reb Mordechai, I'm sorry.") he pauses to answer Reb Mordechai. The question itself does not appear in the Sofer text; from the Rav's answer it seems to have been whether the two Shimons, Shimon ben Shetach and Shimon Kipa, could be one person (reconstructed, not heard). The Rav: it "doesn't really fit, maybe according to one shitah it fits, I didn't think of that, maybe according to one opinion, with the dates of Christianity, of Yeshke, יהושע בן פרחיה. Maybe it fits, maybe it doesn't, that's also something to go [look into]. Very interesting. I didn't see anybody that says that. I saw שמעון בר יונה. Could be, could be, could be."

Reason One — We Have More Time

Reb Chezkel offers the simplest answer: we have more time. The Rav likes it. It is the same reason offered earlier for all the additions to Shabbos Pesukei DeZimra.

A Daily Tefillah for Pious Individuals

The Rav then points out that, essentially, we should say Nishmas every day. The Rishonim write that in their times there were Chasidim, pious people, who said Nishmas every single day. This fits, the Rav says, with the Machzor Vitry's claim that it was said in lieu of Birchos HaShachar, a daily tefillah.[22] That is why people who go to the Kosel say it daily, and the Rav adds that Rav Yitzchak's wife says it every day. The name of Rebbetzin Kolodetzky comes up, and the Rav asks, "Rav Chaim Kanievsky's daughter, you mean?" She also says it every day; the Rav jokes "I'm not joining her minyan, now I know." "A whole group of people say Nishmas every day."

Reason Two — The Neshamah Yeseirah

Reb Chezkel offers another reason (the Rav: "Reb Chezkel says moradik"). Listen to the first words: נִשְׁמַת כָּל חַי ... וְרוּחַ כָּל בָּשָׂר. On an ordinary day, every day, it is ruach kol basar, our nefesh davening to Hashem. On Shabbos we have a neshamah yeseirah (נשמה יתירה), an additional soul.[23] So on Shabbos we say Nishmas to express the thanks of the neshamah yeseirah itself for the fact that we have it.

Reason Three — Yetzias Mitzrayim

The Rav adds that "some Rishonim, the Tur I think, write that since Shabbos is a זכר ליציאת מצרים, and we just said Shirat HaYam (אז ישיר is Yetzias Mitzrayim), right after it we say Nishmas on Shabbos to commemorate Yetzias Mitzrayim."[24] "That's another peshat in the Rishonim why we say it on Shabbos."

When Did It Enter the Seder?

The Rav is honest that he is "confused as to when they started saying it." What he can say is this. He reports two views. Tosafos in Pesachim[25] says that they used to say it in the times of Chazal, whereas in the siddurim of the Geonim it is said that in the times of Chazal it was not said every Shabbos; only individuals (יחידים) said it. The tzibbur began saying it as part of the siddur only in the days of the Geonim. What is clear is that Klal Yisrael accepted all of Nishmas, with its different parts composed at different times, and we say it every Shabbos.

Why do we also say it on Yom Tov? "I don't know." A participant pointed out that the Nusach Sefard text, with all the lines discussed above, does not really fit Nusach Ashkenaz, which leaves out some of it. The Rav: "Just like anything else, I'm not sure about that exactly, why the difference between Nusach Ashkenaz and Nusach Sefard."

XI. Nishmas as the Shir HaShirim of Davening — and the Chasidic Experience

The Rokeach, according to the Rav, writes that just as in Tanach the most important song is Shir HaShirim, "Kodesh Kodashim" (the Rav cites "the Mishnah in Maseches Yadayim"), so too in davening. All of davening is holy, but Nishmas is the Shir HaShirim of tefillah.[26] The entire Tanach is called holy, and Shir HaShirim is the holy of holies, the holiest and most sacred song we sing. That, says the Rav, is why the Chasidim treat Nishmas as they do. The Rav himself davened in a Chasidic shul for ten years. "Nishmas is an experience. Nishmas is dramatic. People scream. They say every word, and it is slow." Satmar Chasidim, he says, come down there and say every word, slowly. "I don't know how much kavanah they have, but mishtachavim" (they bow). Nishmas, in his words, is the most expressive piece of liturgy in our siddur that thanks Hashem. He learned to say Nishmas like that, and "I love saying Nishmas like that."

Reb Moshe Wolfson

Reb Yisroel Dovid had just mentioned that in "the Munies" ("I'm calling them the Munies"), in Emunas Yisrael, Reb Moshe Wolfson, זכר צדיק לברכה, they say Nishmas for a half hour on Shabbos morning, which is why they finish davening so late. The Rav says he can confirm it from personal experience. When the Rav was a rabbi in Miami, Reb Moshe Wolfson, זצ"ל, a great tzaddik, came to Miami every winter for two or three months and davened in the Rav's shul.[27] The davening began at nine on Shabbos morning, but he arrived a half hour early. The Rav always wondered what he was doing so early. He went to the mikveh ("obviously," the Rav assumes), and then began Pesukei DeZimra on his own, so that he would not fall behind, because Nishmas took him a half hour.

In a Chasidic shul the Rebbe himself davens Pesukei DeZimra; the Rav's shul was not Chasidic, but he, as the Rav, led Pesukei DeZimra each week, and so he stood right next to Reb Moshe at the mizrach. "I promise you, I never saw a Jew say Nishmas like he did." He was already eighty-nine or ninety, and his whole body was quivering, with tears streaming down his face. He said every word clearly: עַד הֵנָּה עֲזָרוּנוּ רַחֲמֶיךָ. "I truly felt the inspiration that I felt. I felt like I'm standing next to a malach, an angel who really understands every word and thanks Hashem for every breath," for רוּחַ וּנְשָׁמָה שֶׁנָּפַחְתָּ בְּאַפֵּינוּ. It made an unbelievable impact on the way the Rav says Nishmas. (The Rav adds that at the beis medrash of Harborview he has to rush it a bit, and takes a reminder to finish on time as "tochachas tzibbur, chas v'shalom.")

The Yesod VeShoresh HaAvodah

The Rav adds a source from the other camp. The Rav says: "It comes from the Rokeach; one of the Rishonim writes that Nishmas, more than the other Pesukei DeZimra, is to be said b'kol ram u'bin'imah," with a loud voice and in song. (The sentence does not make clear whether the Rokeach himself is the Rishon who writes this; see note 28.) And the sefer Yesod VeShoresh HaAvodah by Rabbi Alexander Ziskind of Grodno (רבי אלכסנדר זיסקינד מגרודנא), about 200 years ago (the Rav: "Horodna"), was written by one of the great Litvish mekubalim, who "wasn't Chasidish" and whom the Chasidim tried to grab for themselves; the Rav thinks he was a student of "the Pnei Yehoshua, maybe."[28] He traveled three times to the Maggid of Mezritch, the main disciple of the Baal Shem Tov, who told him, in the Rav's telling: "I'm not for you. We don't want you by the Chasidim. You're so Chasidish that we want you, like Shimon Kipa, by the Misnagdim," to get them a little excited about avodas Hashem the way the Baal Shem Tov wanted. So he wrote a sefer that is "practically a Chasidic sefer," urging excitement about every part of davening.

On Nishmas, the Rav says, he writes something he writes about no other tefillah. A Jew when he says Nishmas should feel a simchah greater than anything else in the world could give him. "What's your biggest taanug? What's your biggest taavah? The simchah of saying Nishmas should be much higher than that. That is how a Jew is supposed to feel when he says Nishmas." For the Rav it comes down to this: "Thank Hashem for every moment, for every breath."

Forty Days at the Kosel

The Rav returns to the practice noted earlier. In Eretz Yisrael people go to the Kosel for forty days in a row and recite Nishmas, in order to find a shidduch. "You know this all over in Israel." It is a new thing, he says; the mekubalim tell people to do it. Nishmas, he adds, is "obviously a very chashuv tefillah."

XII. Shochen Ad — Where the Shliach Tzibbur Descends

The shiur closes with the ending of Nishmas. On Shabbos we end Nishmas with שׁוֹכֵן עַד, and that is the point at which the shliach tzibbur descends to the amud. On Yom Tov the shliach tzibbur begins at הָאֵל בְּתַעֲצֻמוֹת עֻזֶּךָ, and on Yom Kippur at הַמֶּלֶךְ.[29] Why is that so? The Rav says that he looked into it, and that the answer is Kabbalah. He adds: "I don't know." He mentions that, according to some nuschaos, the Rishonim already discuss it. "It's confusing, complicated." He ends there because it is 10:45 and late, and he notes that there are a couple more things to say.

For Next Shabbos

Next time you say Nishmas, have in mind the two lines the Chida connects to Reb Kloynimus: הַמֵּשִׂיחַ אִלְּמִים and הַמְפַעְנֵחַ נֶעֱלָמִים. Notice where the acrostics hide (יצחק and רבקה in בְּפִי יְשָׁרִים). And remember the one layer after another that Klal Yisrael accepted whole: the Seder night's Birchas HaShir, the rain-prayer's וְאִלּוּ פִינוּ, a miracle in Yerushalayim, and a name hidden in the paragraphs, whether Shimon ben Shetach or the other Shimon.

Have a wonderful chodesh and a wonderful week, and we should be זוכה to thank הקדוש ברוך הוא for everything that we do.

רב חנניה בן עקשיא אומר, רצה הקדוש ברוך הוא לזכות את ישראל, לפיכך הרבה להם תורה ומצות, שנאמר ה' חפץ למען צדקו, יגדיל תורה ויאדיר.
